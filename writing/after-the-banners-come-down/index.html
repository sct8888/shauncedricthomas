After the banners
come down.

We show up and we mean it. Then life moves on, and the real work is whether we move with it or leave the issue behind.

Last month I found myself thinking about 2020 again.

George Floyd was killed, the video spread, and people marched in cities around the world. For a while it felt like everyone finally agreed that something long ignored had to be faced. Watching from Cape Town, it did not feel far away. Different country, different police, same basic question: whose life gets treated as urgent, and whose life gets treated as a number that can wait?

The energy was real. People showed up. Companies and workplaces started saying things they had not said the month before. Then the noise faded. Not because the facts changed, but because attention is limited, and limited attention moves on to the next thing.

This September I saw a version of that same pattern closer to home.

Since July, nine women have been found dead in Ekurhuleni, including a runner who left home and never came back. There were memorial runs, quiet gatherings in Cape Town, a vigil at UCT, and men walking through Ekurhuleni saying they had had enough. The Uyinene Mrwetyana Foundation said women should be able to live, walk, run, and exist without fear. Officials called it a crisis. It was already a crisis. The latest deaths just made it harder to look away.

I am glad people are in the street. I am glad men are showing up in public, not only online. I am also left with a quieter question, the same one 2020 left me with.

What happens in a month? What happens in two? What happens when the investigation is still open, the funerals are over, and the country has found something else to care about?

Gender-based violence in South Africa is not a short spike. It is an ongoing condition. We have called femicide a national disaster, and the killings still keep coming. The problem is not that we never care. The problem is that we care in bursts. A burst feels like action. It is not the same as change.

A burst of care feels like action. It is not the same as change.

I worry about a similar pattern with race, including in rooms where people believe the hard work is already done.

Some diversity conversations treat a quiet week as proof that everyone is having the same experience. If nothing loud happened, the thinking goes, then things must be fine. That is a comfortable story. It is not always a true one.

My day-to-day experience of race is different from people who can treat it as a topic they visit now and then. I do not need a headline to know that. I also do not need everyone to have lived my life in order for us to work well together. I do need space in the conversation for the idea that my reality is not just me overreacting to the news. Some things stay true after the post stops circulating.

I am not writing this to accuse any one person. I am writing it because I have watched the same arc twice now: the 2020 energy, and then this month’s energy around women who should have come home.

We show up. We march. We say the names. We mean it. Then the month turns, and the question is whether our behaviour turned with it.

I do not have a neat plan to offer. I care less about tidy endings than I used to. What I can do is refuse the easy story that says we paid attention, so the problem must be handled.

If you marched this month, I am with you. If you posted, I understand why. If you felt, for a few days, that the country was finally looking, I felt it too.

What I am asking of myself for October is smaller and harder than a banner. Stay with it after the banner comes down. Stay with the women this does not pause for. Stay with colleagues whose reality does not reset when the meeting agenda moves on. Stay curious about the gap between a moment of national feeling and a life that never gets a quieter month.

That gap is where these things usually fade. It is also the only place they actually change.

Shaun Cedric Thomas
