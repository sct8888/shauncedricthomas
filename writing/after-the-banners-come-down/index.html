After the banners
come down.

We activate. We march. We mean it. Then the month turns, and the real question begins.

Last month I went back to 2020.

George Floyd. The video. The marches. The sudden global agreement that something had been true for a long time and could no longer be treated as background. From Cape Town it did not feel foreign. It felt familiar. Different country, different uniform, same question about whose life gets treated as urgent and whose life gets treated as a statistic that can wait.

The energy was real. People activated. People marched. Workplaces found language they had not used the month before. Then, as these things do, the volume dropped. Not because the underlying facts changed. Because attention is a scarce resource, and scarce resources move on.

This September I watched a version of that energy return, closer to home.

The bodies of nine women found in Ekurhuleni since July. A runner who left home and did not come back. Memorial runs. Silent gatherings in Cape Town. A vigil at UCT. Men walking through Ekurhuleni saying they had had enough. The Uyinene Mrwetyana Foundation saying women deserve to live, walk, run and exist without fear. Official language calling it a crisis, which it already was before the latest cluster made it impossible to look past.

I am glad people are in the street. I am glad men are showing up in public, not only in comments. I am also carrying a quieter question, the same one 2020 left me with.

What happens in a month? What happens in two? What happens when the investigation is still open, the funerals are over, and the country has found something else to be urgent about?

Gender-based violence in South Africa is not a spike. It is a condition. The femicide rate has been treated as a national disaster and still arrives, week after week, as if the declaration itself were the work. The pattern is not that we never care. The pattern is that we care in bursts. Bursts feel like action. They are not the same as change.

Bursts feel like action. They are not the same as change.

I have a similar concern about race, including in rooms that believe they have already done the work.

There is a version of diversity conversation that assumes the absence of an incident is the same as the absence of a reality. If nothing explosive happened this week, the thinking goes, then the people in the room must be having roughly the same month. That is a comfortable reading. It is not always a true one.

I live in a different daily texture than people who can treat race as a topic they visit. I do not need a headline to know that. I also do not need everyone to have had my life in order to work well together. I do need the conversation to leave room for the possibility that my reality is not an overreaction to a news cycle. Some things remain true after the post stops circulating.

I am not writing this as a verdict on any one person. I am writing it because I have now watched the same arc twice in a short span of reflection. First the 2020 energy, then this month’s energy around women who should have come home.

We activate. We march. We say the names. We mean it.

Then the month turns, and the question is whether anything in our actual behaviour turned with it.

I do not have a clean programme to offer. I am less interested in resolved than I used to be. What I can do is refuse the easy story: that attention was paid, therefore the thing was handled.

If you marched this month, I am with you. If you posted, I understand why. If you felt, for a few days, that the country was finally looking, I felt it too.

The work I am asking of myself for October is smaller and harder than a banner. Stay with it after the banner is gone. Stay with the women this does not pause for. Stay with the colleagues whose reality does not reset when the agenda moves to the next item. Stay curious about the gap between a moment of national feeling and a life that does not get a quieter month.

That gap is where these things usually fade. It is also the only place they actually change.

Shaun Cedric Thomas
